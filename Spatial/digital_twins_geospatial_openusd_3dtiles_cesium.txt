[0:02] Hello everyone. My name is Sean Lilly and I'm going to be talking about how USD and 3D Tiles come together to create geospatially rich digital twins.

[0:15] First, a little background about Cesium. At Cesium we're building an open platform for 3D geospatial, and our specialty is rendering massive digital representations of the real world. We do a lot with open source: we released CesiumJS in 2010, a web-based JavaScript library for virtual globes; 10 years later Cesium for Unreal, also open source; soon after Cesium for Unity; and most recently Cesium for Omniverse. We're also big on open standards — we're co-creators of glTF (CesiumJS was one of the first loaders of glTF), and we're the creators of 3D Tiles, which I'll be talking about. We're based in Philadelphia. Here's a screenshot of a 3D representation of City Hall.

[1:05] For the talk, the basic points I want to cover are: the importance of geospatial data for digital twins, an overview of 3D Tiles, how we use 3D Tiles in USD and put it all together in Cesium for Omniverse, and finish with some ideas for USD improvements.

[1:26] SIGGRAPH 2022 was our first deep dive into USD. My main task at the conference was to learn as much as possible about USD — we were about to start working on Cesium for Omniverse, which is built on USD. What was really interesting was that USD was pretty much everywhere, and almost everywhere at 2023 as well. It was being used for more than just animation — we saw it in a lot of real-world applications, especially in the architecture, engineering, and construction (AEC) space. That was interesting to us because we're very big on 3D geospatial, and we thought: how can we bring geospatial data into this ecosystem?

[2:10] When I talk about geospatial data, there are a few different forms. First, point clouds — typically captured by LiDAR scanners at very high resolution; individual points may have color, intensity, classification, and other attributes. Here this point cloud is styled based on classification. We also have 3D buildings — this is from our Cesium OpenStreetMap Buildings dataset, which has global coverage of the world's buildings. Then reality models, typically produced from photogrammetry software — very high resolution textured meshes, typically a centimeter or better. We have vector data — points, lines, and polygons draped on the surface — and voxel data; here the screenshot is time-dynamic temperature data of the Gulf of Mexico. We also have instance models such as this Philly Tree Maps dataset. And finally global imagery and terrain.

[3:07] The common theme across all these datasets is that they are massive scale — on the order of gigabytes to terabytes to petabytes depending on the dataset. This means you can't fit it on a single device; you need some sort of level-of-detail system and a streaming system. That's what I'll cover: how 3D Tiles solves that, and how we bring it into USD and Omniverse.

[3:36] Now, why is geospatial context so important for digital twins? Geospatial data helps designers make better decisions using real-world data. Say you're designing a new building in CAD software and you've exported it to USD — often what you see is your design model in a void, an empty space. But what you really want is to place this design model in the context of the real world.

[4:05] Here's a diagram of what this looks like. We have all these DCC tools being exported as USD, then (for this screenshot) the Google Maps Platform 3D Tiles API coming in as 3D Tiles, and both the USD and the 3D Tiles come together into Omniverse to create different layers that produce this result. The whole point is that you can see where it actually is in space, but you can also run simulations — for example shadow studies to see how this building casts shadows onto other buildings, or how other buildings cast shadows onto it. This can impact the decisions you make; you might notice something you didn't realize before and go back and edit the design model. You can also run other sorts of simulations.

[4:53] I'll show two videos of projects we've worked on in collaboration with NVIDIA, to show larger-scale digital twin projects. The first is this one. We can look at an ICON simulation of any part of the world at a resolution of 1.25 kilometers. Here you can see the detail in the clouds over Taiwan, or thunderstorms over Africa that form as the sun rises and its light strikes the clouds. Typical simulations today are done at 25-kilometer resolution and simply can't show these details. Observing the climate in high resolution at a global scale is powerful, but it's also important to show the impacts to specific regions or even specific areas of a city. Here's a simulation of Ernst-Reuter-Platz in Berlin, where we can see how the size and location of buildings impact airflows, based on data from PALM and volumetric data from ICON. This gives architects and city planners insights into how they can design and build smarter, more sustainable cities, while letting climate scientists gain a deeper understanding of our changing world. That was the Earth-2 demo shown at the GTC conference recently.

[6:21] The next video is something we built earlier. NVIDIA, Cesium, and the U.S. Space Force are partnering to build a digital twin of the Cape Canaveral Spaceport. The goal is to help accomplish operational unification across private entities such as SpaceX, ULA, and Blue Origin, and several government-affiliated entities including NASA, Space Force, and Air Force. NVIDIA Omniverse, built on the open standard Universal Scene Description, enables data interoperability and faster development between 3D and simulation tools and data types such as 3D Tiles, ultimately increasing situational awareness. Here Omniverse is being used to simulate hazard areas around a rocket launch. Flight caution areas are described by geolocated points, and the ground terrain is produced by a plug-in connecting Omniverse to Cesium's 3D Tiles service. For even more accurate simulation, live telemetry from transponders or radar systems drives multiple objects in the synchronized digital twin. Here we see transponder data from air traffic moving a vehicle in the Omniverse digital twin, allowing visual confirmation that the plane is outside of the hazard zones. Omniverse enables connection across vendor and software silos, letting teams experience the digital twin in their preferred tool, such as this Unity-based application. Omniverse directly connects to the rocket telemetry driving the motion of the vehicle, as seen here with the visualization of the launch based on telemetry data connected to USD. Unifying all the demands is difficult, but by leveraging Omniverse's open standards and extensibility, developers can connect Omniverse to applications such as Cesium and Unity. This enables Omniverse to tie together all ground, seaport, and space launch information into a full-fidelity, precise-time digital twin, and helps realize the efficient utilization of the spaceport's finite resources.

[8:46] Now that we've looked at some use cases, I want to talk more about 3D Tiles. 3D Tiles is an open standard for massive 3D geospatial data. It was started by Cesium in 2015 and became an OGC Community Standard in 2019. One way to think about 3D Tiles is that it provides a spatial subdivision for glTF. It has a concept called hierarchical level of detail: you have a tree of tiles where your root tile is the least detailed version, its children are more detailed, and the leaf tiles are the highest resolution. Each tile is a glTF model. The advantage is that you only have to stream what you need for a given view — you don't need the whole dataset in memory; based on your camera parameters you can load the tiles that you need.

[9:36] To break down the difference between 3D Tiles and glTF: 3D Tiles provides the spatial subdivision — it has a bounding volume hierarchy and a concept called geometric error, which determines when a tile refines to its children. Each tile is a glTF, which has the geometry and textures. It also has a bunch of compression extensions that glTF provides, such as Draco and meshopt for geometry, and KTX2 / Basis Universal for texture compression. You can also have additional information as part of your glTF — feature identification to say which vertices or texels represent windows versus doors — and metadata assigned to those features.

[10:24] Looking at 3D Tiles and USD and how they come together — there's still a lot to explore, but I've broken out the strengths of each. 3D Tiles is really massive scale, designed for large geospatial datasets, built for streaming, and built for runtime efficiency, trying to get to the GPU as fast as possible. USD is excellent at non-destructive editing, composition, and having a flexible scene description.

[10:52] So how do we bring 3D Tiles and USD together into a common view? The pipeline looks like this. At the top left is the Cesium ion section — this is where you take your source geospatial data, whether that's OBJ models, LAS point clouds, or GeoTIFF for terrain and imagery and other datasets. That source data gets processed into 3D Tiles; we have domain-specific tilers and simplification algorithms. These get saved in S3 that we stream out. There's also a variety of pre-packaged geospatial datasets that we host — Cesium World Terrain, Cesium OSM Buildings, and Bing imagery — and other data providers such as the Google Maps Platform serving 3D Tiles for their 3D data. Even if you just have 3D Tiles on local disk, you can use that.

[11:40] The way the code works: we have a library called Cesium Native, a C++ open-source library for streaming 3D Tiles. It's engine-agnostic, so we use it for all our different runtimes — Cesium for Unreal, Cesium for Unity, and Cesium for Omniverse. Cesium for Omniverse is an extension on top of NVIDIA Omniverse; 3D Tiles gets streamed into Omniverse with the extension, and then we have our DCC tools that are also going to Omniverse via USD.

[12:10] One of the big challenges when dealing with 3D Tiles and USD is coordinate systems. 3D Tiles is typically a WGS84 representation of the world — a very large range, so ~6 million meters. It's a right-handed coordinate system, and there's really no up direction, because it depends on where you are on the earth what's considered up. USD is also a right-handed coordinate system; it can be Y-up or Z-up, and the unit size is configurable, with the default being centimeters. So the order of transformations: we go from glTF, do a few transforms, but eventually have to convert the global coordinate system of 3D Tiles into a local coordinate system. That's where this fixed-frame-to-ENU (East-North-Up) conversion comes in. It takes a point — a longitude/latitude you want — and that becomes your tangent plane and the center of your world, the local coordinate system. Once you have that, you can place your USD assets in this local space, and you can even anchor them based on longitude/latitude so they stay at the same spot.

[13:18] Looking at the runtime details: even though 3D Tiles streaming and USD come in a little bit separately, we still use USD a lot inside our extension. We use USD to represent the scene description of the tilesets — for example, we have this one tileset on screen, and that would be a Cesium tileset Prim object inside USD that inherits from a Gprim (because we want to assign materials to it and have extents). We have a few other types for top-level metadata about the scene, geo-referencing information, and imagery information. We also heavily use TF notices, so if someone changes, for example, the URL of the dataset, or the longitude/latitude of the geo-reference, we get notified and reload the tileset or make some other change based on that notification.

[14:15] Looking at the renderer architecture: because 3D Tiles packages glTF, we end up having to go from glTF into a renderable form. We have parallel tracks — the USD stage, where all the design models go, and Cesium for Omniverse, which is glTF. We're using the lower-level Fabric scene delegate inside Omniverse, which is a post-composed version of the stage and very efficient. In the end that goes to Hydra, then to the render delegate, then to RTX. On a frame-by-frame basis: first we get the viewport / view-projection matrices, we call update-view in Cesium Native (which tells us which tiles to select), then we convert glTF to Fabric prims, then we set visibility and transforms on those Fabric prims.

[15:08] Our wish list for USD: we're interested in industry-wide geospatial schemas, especially for anchoring objects on the globe — some common way to say "here's the longitude/latitude and my coordinate system." We're also very interested in USD and glTF alignment — first, material interoperability, which is being explored by the 3D Asset Interoperability group at the Metaverse Standards Forum. Another thing is improved point cloud rendering support — even though this isn't necessarily a USD domain (it's more of the rendering domain), there are a lot of use cases for digital twins to have point cloud data. Finally, we'd like to explore more 3D-Tiles-in-USD interoperability. Currently they're loaded separately, but we're thinking: how do we maybe use 3D Tiles to spatially subdivide USD, or take concepts of 3D Tiles and turn them into USD? A lot of areas to explore here — not sure where it's going yet.
