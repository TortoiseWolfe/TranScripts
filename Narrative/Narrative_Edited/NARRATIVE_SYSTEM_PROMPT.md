## 1. Every Story Shares One Structure — Campbell's Monomyth

- **Human society runs on stories.** They create our reality and the way we see the world. It *feels* like there are infinite, all-different stories — but the **basic structure of anything recognizable as a story is the same**. Style is what makes stories different; structure is what they share.
- Many people tried to formalize this; perhaps no one did it better than philosopher **Joseph Campbell**, who developed the **monomyth** — the **Hero's Journey** — in *The Hero with a Thousand Faces*.
- It wasn't a screenwriting book. Campbell, through **comparative mythology**, noticed that parables about Christ resembled Native American folktales that predated Christ and had no contact with Christian culture.
- His conclusion: the **characteristics of an effective story are consistent regardless of religion, race, time, or ancestry.** Mythology, in his view, "is pop music, it's the dream you're describing to your friend on the subway, it's drawings on a napkin, it's basically everything."

## 2. The Story Circle — Eight Steps

**Dan Harmon** simplified the monomyth into the **Story Circle**, which he believes is **universal for any story in any medium**. Draw a circle with a **horizontal line** through it (top = ordinary world, bottom = special world) and a **vertical line** down the center, creating four crossing points (numbered 1, 3, 5, 7) and four quadrants (numbered 2, 4, 6, 8), walked clockwise from the top:

| # | Step | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **YOU** | A character is in a zone of comfort. |
| 2 | **NEED** | They want something. |
| 3 | **GO** | They enter an unfamiliar situation. |
| 4 | **SEARCH** | They adapt to it. |
| 5 | **FIND** | They get what they wanted. |
| 6 | **TAKE** | They pay a heavy price for it. |
| 7 | **RETURN** | They return to their familiar situation… |
| 8 | **CHANGE** | …having changed. |

**The two axes:**
- **Horizontal (top ↔ bottom) — the external struggle.** Crossing this line means the hero **enters a new situation and is forced to adapt**, usually struggling against an external force. This is the **ordinary world vs. the special world**; size doesn't matter, but there must be **contrast** between the two.
- **Vertical (left ↔ right) — the inner struggle.** Crossing this line is where the hero finally faces and tries to **overcome their inner flaw**. In simplest terms: **order / chaos, stasis / change.** (*Die Hard*: top-to-bottom is a failing marriage and a terrorist attack; left-to-right is stubborn → not stubborn — once McClane descends and returns, he can fix the marriage.)

## 3. The Three Dualities & the Descent-and-Return Rhythm

Harmon says every story has a rhythm explained by **three dualities**:

- **Life and death.** You're alive, then dead; your body feeds plants, giving new life that then dies. "That's a story — it goes back and forth." The two rely on each other; **balance is what lets things happen.**
- **Consciousness and unconsciousness.** Upstairs the conscious mind is comfortable, well-lit, regularly swept; downstairs the unconscious is older, darker, freakier. Sanity and life **depend on occasional round trips** — the ego ventures into the unconscious via therapy, meditation, confession, sex, violence, or **a good story** — to keep the conscious mind in working order.
- **Order and chaos.** Just as a person's health depends on the ego's descent and return, a **society's longevity depends on people journeying into the unknown and returning with ideas.** In their most dramatic form these people are **heroes**.

The payoff: **all stories follow this pattern of descent and return, of diving and emerging.** All life — the human mind, the communities we create — marches to the same beat. If a story marches to that beat, **it resonates**: it sends the audience's ego on a brief trip to the unconscious and back, and **"the audience has an instinctive taste for that, and they're going to say, 'Yum.'"**

## 4. Worked Example — *Star Wars: A New Hope*

Schoder maps the circle beat-by-beat onto *A New Hope*:

| Step | Beat in *A New Hope* |
|---|---|
| **1 YOU** | Establish the protagonist — fade in on Luke; "we are them until we have a better choice." |
| **2 NEED** | Something's off balance — the droids and Leia's message: "You must learn the ways of the Force… if you were to come with me to Alderaan." |
| **3 GO** | Crossing the threshold — "There's nothing for me here now. I want to learn the ways of the Force and become a Jedi like my father." |
| **4 SEARCH** | The road of trials — escape from the Death Star's tractor beam and pursuit; Campbell's "digestive tract" strips the hero of fear and desire ("nothing can save you here"). |
| **5 FIND** | Meeting with the goddess — "I'm Luke Skywalker. I'm here to rescue you." The NEED is fulfilled (even if not as expected); the point of greatest vulnerability, where the hero makes a choice and descends. |
| **6 TAKE** | Meet your maker — full control over destiny; **the price of the journey** paired with a goal achieved you never knew you had. |
| **7 RETURN** | Bringing it home — "Analysis of the plans provided by Princess Leia has demonstrated a weakness in the battle station." "It's not a journey if you never come back." |
| **8 CHANGE** | Master of both worlds — the Death Star is blown up; the YOU is back in charge but is now a *situation changer*. |

## 5. Applying It — A Tool, Not a Cage

- The circle **applies to any piece of a story.** A sitcom writer (e.g., *Community*) writes **a separate circle for each character** — how each one traverses the story and changes top-to-bottom, left-to-right, as it moves forward.
- Is it the only way to make a story? **No** — but Harmon believes all stories come back to this structure, **otherwise they're not recognizable as stories.**
- Deconstructing storytelling this way shouldn't make it feel dull or repetitive. Harmon's own work is **proof that dedicated storytellers can use this basic structure to tell incredibly complex, subversive, and consistently meaningful stories in their own style.**
- Great stories **keep us psychologically, spiritually, and socially alive** — fine-tuning us to the rhythm of life and death, conscious and unconscious, order and chaos. "A great story can change someone's life. So get out there and tell yours."

## Prompt Patterns

- **Map a story:** "Map my story idea to the eight-step Story Circle — where does each beat fall?"
- **Gap-finding:** "Which step is my draft missing — is there a real GO, a real price at TAKE, an actual CHANGE?"
- **Rhythm critique:** "Critique this outline's descent-and-return rhythm. Does it send the audience down and back, or does it stall?"
- **Character circles:** "Write a separate Story Circle for each character in this ensemble."
- **Beat classification:** "Is this beat the FIND (meeting the goddess) or the TAKE (paying the price)? Why?"
- **Axis check:** "Map the external struggle (top/bottom) and the inner struggle (left/right) for my protagonist."
